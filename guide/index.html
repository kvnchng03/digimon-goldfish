Eight habits to build.

Each lesson has an example and a short drill. These are coaching tools; the best line depends on your cards and opponent.

Build an opening with a jobWin condition & consistency

Describe how your deck wins in one sentence: a large attack turn, several small attackers, or surviving until the opponent runs out of resources. Then name the setup it needs. A hand is useful when it starts that plan and has a route to the next step.

Try this comparison

In a deck that needs a Tamer to function, an evolution line without Tamer access can be weaker than a less complete line with an early engine and search. In another deck, that same Tamer may be too slow. Judge cards by their jobs, not just their levels.

Five-minute drill: Deal ten opening hands. Before drawing anything else, say keep or redraw, your first two plays, and the card you still need. Track whether the opening plan worked, not whether the game was won.

Plan both sides of the memory gaugeTempo & useful resources

Card advantage is having more useful cards to work with. Tempo is what those resources let you accomplish now. A search that adds cards can still be too slow if it funds the opponent's winning turn.

Price the whole line

Start at 3 memory. Spend 2: you have 1. Spend another 3: the gauge reaches their 2. This assumes no discounts or memory changes. Compare the board you built with what they can do from there; a Tamer that sets them to 3 may erase your denial.

Crossing the gauge does not interrupt an attack or unresolved effects. Check the end-of-turn conditions after processing, including any end-of-turn effects. Official rules: §§6-1-4, 6-6.

Five-minute drill: Replay one turn with two lines. Write the memory after every cost and gain, the final board, and the opponent's likely start. Explain which line gives them the less dangerous reply.

Sequence to keep your options openInformation & breeding timing

When two actions are legal, ask whether one gives information that could change the other. Draws and searches can guide a later commitment, but only if you leave enough memory and the required targets to use what you find.

Make the decision at the right time

A search might determine which evolution you choose in your main phase. It usually cannot inform that turn's normal breeding-phase move: that phase already happened. Decide whether to expose the stack using the information you have then.

Holding a stack can protect your investment; moving it can apply pressure and free breeding for your next stack. Compare the value you get now, the removal you expect, and the cost of waiting. Don't turn “stay safe” into an automatic rule.

Timing reference: official rules, §§6-4 and 6-5.

Five-minute drill: Take a hand and board you remember. List two legal orders of play. Circle where a new card changes your choice and where you commit a resource you cannot get back.

Count a complete finishAttacks, security & risk

Count legal attacks, not just bodies. Check who can attack, memory, DP, inherited effects and every cost needed to attack again. Then account for blockers, redirects, counter effects, recovery and security that can stop the line.

Three security is not three attacks for game

Against 3 security, an attack making 2 checks, then one making 1 check, then a separate direct attack can win. Assume all attacks connect, the checking attackers survive their checks, and there are no interruptions or recovery. Extra checks alone do not turn the attack that empties security into a win.

The final attacker must still be able to perform at least one security check. A blocker can consume an attack you needed for the finish; removing it may cost memory needed elsewhere. Official rules: §§11-5, 12-13.

Before taking a partial attack, ask whether it brings your winning turn closer. Security may give them resources or remove your attacker. If you cannot survive another turn, accepting that risk can still be your best chance.

Five-minute drill: Lay out a possible finish, then add one blocker. Recount from the beginning. Name which security effect breaks the line and whether you can preserve a backup attacker.

Learn their next important turnMatchups & racing

Your clock is how soon you threaten to win if the position holds. Compare it with theirs. If they threaten to win first, you need a faster line or a way to interrupt them. If you are ahead, consider which response could take that lead away.

Learn a matchup in five questions

  • What setup lets them win?
  • What can they do on their next turn?
  • Which removal, blockers or protection matter?
  • How do they gain memory or resources from security?
  • Which of my cards answers their key threat?

Read the actual source, timing, scope and duration of protection. A remembered label like “immune” is not enough to choose an answer. Include revealed cards, Tamers and the stack in breeding in your prediction.

During your next game: Predict their main play before each opposing turn. Afterwards, compare what happened and the information you missed. Ask your practice partner which alternative they considered.

Count the cards that actually helpOuts & uncertainty

An out is a card or sequence that saves or wins the position. Name what it must do and whether you can afford it in time. A card that answers a threat on paper is not a live out if you cannot use it this turn.

Four in the list is not four left to draw

You started with four copies of an answer. Two are in trash and one is in your hand. At most one remains elsewhere; it is not necessarily in your deck. The card in hand may already solve the problem, and trash access may make the discarded copies useful.

Use your hand and public information to update the count. Don't treat unknown security as known draws, assume an unseen opposing card is absent, or use exact odds without stating what locations and access your model includes.

Five-minute drill: Write every answer to one difficult board, its cost, and the copies you can still access. Estimate your chance of finding one, then compare with the workbook's “Find my out” model and its assumptions.

Review the decision before the resultFeedback & deliberate practice

Look earlier than the final attack. Reconstruct the first decision that meaningfully changed your chances: the hand you kept, the stack you exposed, the resource you spent, or the threat you ignored. Review wins too; a good result can hide a poor line.

Separate risk from hindsight

A security effect stopped your attack. That does not automatically make attacking wrong. Compare attacking and waiting with the information available before the flip. If waiting almost certainly loses, the risky attack may be the stronger decision.

Record the position, your chosen line, a legal alternative, and why you preferred one. Multiple causes can contribute to a loss. If you cannot reconstruct enough information, mark the conclusion as uncertain.

After your next session: Review one win and one loss. Pick one recurring decision to drill. In your next games, track whether you applied that habit, alongside the results.

Change the list with a reasonTesting & deckbuilding

Keep one list for a block of practice so you can learn its lines. Before swapping cards, name the problem: missing setup, running out of resources, failing to answer a threat, or not finishing. A piloting mistake may need a drill rather than a new card.

Make a testable claim

“Replacing one expensive finisher with one searcher should give me more playable openings, but may weaken late-game draws.” Track both outcomes across similar matchups and turn orders. Record the list version so you know which games belong together.

Change one idea at a time and note what you gave up. A short winning streak does not establish that a swap is better. Repeated awkward hands can suggest a hypothesis; they do not prove the cause by themselves.

Before your next edit: Write the expected benefit, the tradeoff, and what evidence would make you undo it. Review actual decisions as well as the win-loss record.

A week of useful practice.

Repeat this cycle with one focus. Short sessions count.

  1. Prepare: 10 minutes

    Choose a lesson. Drill ten opening hands or replay one turn with exact memory and attack counts.

  2. Play: one focused session

    Keep the same list. Predict the opponent's next turn and note where your chosen habit changed a decision.

  3. Review: 10 minutes

    Reconstruct one win and one loss. Pause a recorded game at a similar decision, choose a line, then compare the player's reasoning.

  4. Return: next session

    Redo the difficult position without looking at your notes. Keep the focus if it still needs work, or move to the next habit.

Give the next session a purpose.

“Before passing, I will name their most dangerous reply.”

Choose a habit you can observe. “Win more” tells you the result; a specific decision tells you what to practise.

Make every session teach you something.

Stay on one list long enough to separate decisions from deck changes.

  1. Warm up with a few decisions.

    Do 2-3 puzzles. Choose a line before opening the answer.

  2. Play a block on one list.

    Use best-of-3 when possible. Track first/second and how you adapt between games.

  3. Count and predict each turn.

    Memory, fuel, security and DP. Before passing, name their likely next play.

  4. Review the deciding turn.

    Look earlier than the final attack. Separate draw, misplay, matchup and security flip.

  5. Pause a strong player's game.

    Choose your move, resume, then compare the reasoning in the commentary.

  6. Drill the recurring mistake.

    After 20-30 games, look for repeated leaks. Revisit missed puzzles a week later.
